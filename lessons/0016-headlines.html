Why this lesson

One goal in the mission is to read NRC and de Volkskrant without a dictionary. You start a newspaper with the headlines. But headlines are not normal Dutch. They leave out words, and they use tense in their own way. Learn four moves and a headline is a full sentence again. The words you need for news are in the word list.

Ukrainian hook. Ukrainian has no articles, so a Dutch headline without de and het will feel natural to you. That is the trap. Headline Dutch is the only place where Dutch drops them. In an email or a letter, keep every article.

The idea: four moves

1. Drop the articles. Meisje redt kleuter = Een meisje redt een kleuter. If an editor can drop only one, it is usually the first one: Meisje redt een kleuter. Source: research by Joke de Lange on Volkskrant, Telegraaf and NRC headlines, in Onze Taal 2008/11. Onze Taal already called this normal headline practice in 1950.

2. Drop is, zijn and wordt. A past participle on its own means it happened to someone. Man aangehouden = Er is een man aangehouden. Treinverkeer ontregeld = Het treinverkeer is ontregeld.

3. Present tense for news. Yesterday's vote is Kamer stemt in met wet, not stemde in. Read it as "has agreed". The article below the headline switches to the perfect: De Tweede Kamer heeft ingestemd met …

4. A colon means "says". Minister: 'Geen extra geld voor zorg' = De minister zegt: …. Newspapers put a quote in single quotation marks (Onze Taal 2000). Single quotes around one word mean "their word, not ours": Rechter 'beledigd'.

Move 1 and the quotation marks come from the linked Onze Taal articles. Moves 2 and 3 are the teacher's observation of how Dutch papers write; check them yourself on any front page. Also common: the age in brackets, Man (34), read as een man van 34.
