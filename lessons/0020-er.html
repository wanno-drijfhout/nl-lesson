Why this lesson

Er is two letters long and has four jobs. English has no word like it, so learners leave it out. And one missing er gives you away.

Petra Sleeman, a linguist at the University of Amsterdam, puts it plainly: native speakers always say Ik heb er twee gekocht, never Ik heb twee gekocht. A learner can speak perfect Dutch and still door de mand vallen, be found out, by dropping this one word (Sleeman 2022).

You have used er already: ermee in lesson 0019, Wie is er? on the phone. This lesson puts the four jobs side by side.

The idea: four jobs, one word

The big Dutch grammar, the ANS, splits er into four uses. The four ANS examples, as quoted by Van Herpt on Neerlandistiek:

JobWhat it doesANS example
1. Place'there', for a place already namedHoe lang werkt hij er al?
2. Number'of them', next to a number or amountZij heeft er vijf.
3. Preposition'it', glued to over, mee, bij …Hij koopt er een kaartje bij.
4. Up frontannounces a new subject, like 'there is'Vandaag vinden er drie verschillende wedstrijden plaats.

The ANS calls them locatief, kwantitatief, prepositioneel and presentatief. You do not need the names. You need the four shapes.

Job 1, place. Once a place is named, er points back to it. It is never stressed.

Ken je Leiden? Ik heb er gestudeerd.
Ben je er al? 'Are you there yet?'

To stress the place, use daar at the start: Daar heb ik gestudeerd!

The daar tip and the examples outside the table are the teacher's own.

Job 2: er + a number

This is the one that gives learners away. When you leave out the noun after a number, Dutch fills the gap with er.

Hoeveel kinderen heb je? Ik heb er twee.
Die appels zijn lekker. Ik neem er nog drie.
Hoeveel wilt u er?

English says 'I have two'. German says Ich habe zwei. Dutch says Ik heb er twee. Sleeman notes that French has the same word, en: Il en a trois = Hij heeft er drie (Sleeman 2022).

Ukrainian hook. Ukrainian can do this too: Скільки в тебе дітей? — У мене їх двоє. That їх is your er. In Ukrainian you may drop it. In Dutch you may not.

Two Belgian traps. Sleeman cites the ANS on two uses that are common in Belgium but not in the Netherlands (Sleeman 2022):

Ik heb er nog vijftig witte: er with a number and an adjective.
Ja, ik heb er nog: er with no number at all.

In the Netherlands, say Ik neem twee rode and Ja, ik heb nog appels, or Ja, die heb ik nog.

The Netherlands alternatives in the last line are the teacher's own.

Job 3: er + a preposition

Dutch does not say over het or met het for a thing. The het becomes er, and it goes before the preposition:

Not thisBut thisMeaning
over heteroverabout it
met hetermeewith it
van hetervanof it, from it
in heterinin it
bij heterbijwith it, in addition

Note met becomes mee, as in lesson 0019.

The split. Er and the preposition often come apart. Other words go in between:

Ik denk er nog over na. 'I'm still thinking about it.'
Ik heb er geen zin in. 'I don't feel like it.'
Ik hou er echt van. 'I really love it.'

Team Taaladvies gives the rule: when other words stand between er and over, you write them apart. And the split order is often more common than the joined one (Team Taaladvies).

The habit: er goes early, right after the verb and the subject. The preposition waits near the end.

Ukrainian hook. про це, з цим, від цього: Ukrainian keeps the preposition and the pronoun together. Dutch flips them and then pulls them apart. Я думаю про це → Ik denk er … over na.

The 'not this' column, the habit line and the hook are the teacher's own.

Job 4: er up front

When the subject is new, a thing you have not mentioned yet, Dutch does not start with it. It starts with er, like English 'there is':

Er staat een man voor de deur. 'There's a man at the door.'
Er zijn geen aardbeien meer. 'There are no strawberries left.'
Wie is er aan de beurt? 'Whose turn is it?'

But Dutch uses it with more verbs than 'be'. Look at the ANS example again: Vandaag vinden er drie wedstrijden plaats. English would just say 'three matches take place today'.

The subject after this er is new and loose: een, geen, a number, iemand, iets, wie. A subject you already know, with de, het or mijn, goes first without er: Je fiets staat voor de deur.

Two at once. Jobs 4 and 2 can meet: Er zijn er nog drie. 'There are three left.' The first er announces, the second one counts. Both stay.

The rule on new and known subjects and the 'two at once' note are the teacher's own. No source I checked states them in this form.

At the market

Saturday, the weekly market. You are at the fruit stall. Read it aloud, all parts. The last column names the job.

Look at Daar maak je … van: daar does job 3 too, with stress. And Doe er maar … bij = 'add … to it'. The maar is the friendly particle from lesson 0004. The dialogue is the teacher's own.

Market Dutch. At the stall, een pond is 500 grams and een ons is 100 grams. Mag het ietsje meer zijn? means the seller cut a bit too much. Say Ja hoor.
